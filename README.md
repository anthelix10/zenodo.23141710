Code and Data for: **One market-volume cap or one per battery? A formal decomposition of multi-asset battery backtests in Great Britain**

**Preprint**: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7310600

**Zenodo DOI**: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23141710

**Abstract**: Storage-market models address liquidity, price impact and multi-asset aggregation, but may leave one prior choice implicit. The issue is which economic unit receives a common market-volume allowance. We model that choice as a partition of physical assets. A focused review of 15 related formulations found no study that kept the same exogenous allowance while changing the receiving unit. For a fixed feasible schedule, partition refinement cannot increase measured excess, and with a non-negative penalty, the conditional score cannot fall. The fixed-schedule ordering therefore follows analytically, while the resulting score difference and the dispatch response to reoptimisation must be quantified empirically. We evaluate all five partitions of a synthetic three-battery portfolio in Great Britain over 1,081 days (51,888 half-hour periods) in 2023-2025. In the standard endpoint comparison, three 2% per asset caps can total 6% of the same Market Index Volume (MIV), while one aggregate cap remains 2%. Reoptimised scores range from GBP 6.57 million under one aggregate cap to GBP 8.78 million under three per-asset caps. The three two-group partitions yield GBP 7.36-8.27 million. Applying the aggregate audit to the unchanged per-asset schedule lowers the score by GBP 7.42 million, and aggregate reoptimisation recovers GBP 5.21 million. Holding total allowance constant gives aggregate scores of GBP 10.07 million under a 6% cap and GBP 9.95 million under the contemporaneous sum of per-asset caps. These findings suggest that multi-asset battery backtests should explicitly identify the economic unit receiving each allowance, disclose the implied portfolio-level budget, and separate cap-accounting effects from dispatch responses. Capacity and daily boundary sensitivities affect score levels, while netting and binary complementarity leave the reference gap nearly unchanged. The GBP values here are conditional model scores, not realised revenues.

**Discussion**: 

**1. Contribution relative to conventional battery backtests**
The paper draws on four strands of literature. These cover single-device volume constraints, a volume limit on an aggregated fleet, liquidity and price-impact models, and work on aggregation or strategic behaviour across multiple devices. Kaya (2025) is the closest aggregate-fleet volume-limit precedent, limiting an aggregated fleet by available market volume before internal dispatch, while Cognéville et al. (2024) is the closest multi-battery treatment of order-book liquidity. None of the 15 formulations in our focused map apply the same allowance across alternative economic-unit partitions. 
Making the economic-unit partition explicit connects these strands, and repeating the allowance for each asset changes the portfolio's notional budget before dispatch is re-solved, so the per-asset-versus-aggregate comparison is not budget neutral. We combine the fixed-schedule ordering with all five partitions, separate the accounting change from the dispatch response, and add counterfactuals that preserve the total cap. The proposition fixes the structural direction for a common schedule, while the data determines the size of the budget change, the accounting gap, and the amount recovered by reoptimisation. The structural ordering concerns a fixed schedule of non-negative gross directional flows. It neither orders signed net positions nor determines the realised scores of separately optimised schedules.

**2. Implications for multi-asset studies**
Any study that derives participation caps from a common market-volume proxy should state the economic unit receiving each allowance. Depending on the trading arrangement, it may be an asset owner, route-to-market provider or contractual portfolio. Where the mapping is uncertain, plausible partitions should be tested rather than treating one assignment as universal. The two-block cases show that group count alone is insufficient, and that asset identities and ratings also matter. Holding the total cap constant separates joint optimisation from a change in the market-access budget. Studies should also state whether opposite flows are netted and whether an allowance can be transferred between assets. These choices represent different trading arrangements, not interchangeable accounting conventions.
The capacity test shows why scores and grouping rankings cannot simply be scaled by fleet size. The daily energy test shows that a positive score difference can remain even when both score levels change substantially. Netting and preventing simultaneous operation have little effect on the reference difference in this sample, but the trading and physical assumptions still need to be stated.

**3. Scope and limitations**
The reported magnitudes depend on a synthetic three-asset portfolio in which Pillswood supplies most of the energy capacity, a daily 50% start-and-end state-of-charge condition, fixed efficiency, one public matched-volume proxy, selected values of ρ and φ, and an out-of-sample forecast design that was not preregistered. The model excludes outages, ancillary-service commitments, inter-day opportunity costs, bid-ask spreads, imbalance exposure, auxiliary consumption and detailed degradation. The scores should thus not be interpreted as deployable or realised revenue. The literature review is focused rather than systematic, covering 15 formulations closest to the allocation question, but not unpublished code, commercial models or all storage-market papers. It is intended rather to establish whether the exact comparison appears in that set and not to estimate its prevalence or claim universal priority.
The full parameter grid uses the linear model. Tests that prevent simultaneous operation cover the reference comparison and the capacity, netting and daily energy scenarios, not every grid setting. All comparisons retain the same forecasts and earlier volume inputs.
The sensitivity and operational analyses define the boundaries of the main result, and none are presented as a separate headline finding. Alternative forecasts and throughput costs show that the magnitude depends on modelling choices. The PN feasibility exercise shows why operational series need an asset-mapping and energy-consistency check, while moving-block resampling describes stability within the observed history, not structural or population uncertainty. The capacity and daily inventory tests examine specified alternatives, not the true plant configuration or population uncertainty, and a mismatch between an operational series and the model is not proof that an actual plant violated its physical limits.
A natural next step is to replace the exogenous excess penalty with an endogenous model of market-access cost. Under a hard cap, the dual multiplier λ_(G,t) would give the marginal value of relaxing the volume limit for economic unit G. Under a soft cap, a direction-specific cost function calibrated to order-book or route-to-market data should be more informative than the constant penalty φ. The partition framework could also be applied to renewable-storage portfolios where contracts and settlement rules permit internal netting. This extension would allow researchers to test whether diversification reduces external trading and downside revenue risk, and whether it changes the risk-adjusted floor or toll which a portfolio can support. 

**Conclusions**:
Cap assignment is part of a multi-asset backtest's economic specification and not a coding detail. Our focused review of 15 storage-market formulations found no direct comparison that kept the same exogenous allowance while changing the receiving unit. Treating assignment as a partition provides a fixed-schedule ordering and separates the accounting effect from the dispatch response.
The Great Britain case study shows that this choice matters. At the reference setting, reoptimised scores range from GBP 6.57 million under one aggregate cap to GBP 8.78 million under three per-asset caps. The three two-group partitions yield GBP 7.36-8.27 million. Applying the aggregate audit to the unchanged per-asset schedule creates a GBP 7.42 million accounting gap. Reoptimising under the aggregate cap recovers GBP 5.21 million, leaving GBP 2.21 million between the optimised endpoints. Yet the endpoint comparison also changes total allowance. For a three-asset portfolio, separate 2% per-asset caps permit an aggregate allowance of up to 6% of MIV, compared with 2% under a single portfolio-level cap. Preserving the total allowance gives aggregate scores of GBP 10.07 million under a 6% cap and GBP 9.95 million under the contemporaneous sum of per-asset caps. Across 25 parameter settings, the reoptimised difference remains positive at GBP 0.58-3.34 million. Thus aggregation does not mechanically destroy value. Accounting, dispatch and total allowance are distinct and should be reported separately.
All five partitions are reported across the full parameter grid. Changing the assumed capacity can reverse the ranking of two intermediate groupings, and the daily energy requirement affects score levels. Preventing simultaneous operation changes each reference score by less than 0.1%. Netting changes the reference difference little, but is outside the proposition's scope. The GBP values are conditional model scores, not realised revenues.
Separate caps may suit independently traded assets. One aggregate cap may suit assets sharing an optimiser, route-to-market arrangement or contract. A defensible backtest should name the unit receiving each allowance, disclose the implied portfolio budget, test plausible partitions when the mapping is uncertain, and report accounting separately from dispatch. The appropriate partition will vary by context, but leaving it implicit can make a model appear to create value, because the same market-access allowance has been counted more than once.
